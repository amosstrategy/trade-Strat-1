# America Under Strain: Income, Debt, Health, Fertility — and Who Can Actually Pay for AI Services

**Scope:** United States only  
**Stance:** Skeptical of “everything is fine” narratives; prefer government, central-bank, and bank-institute microdata over marketing claims  
**Date of compilation:** July 2026  
**Currency note:** Price points in the brief were given in euros. For U.S. households we convert at roughly **€1 ≈ $1.10** → **€100 ≈ $110**, **€200 ≈ $220**, **€500 ≈ $550**, **€5,000 ≈ $5,500** per month. Exact FX does not change the order of magnitude.

---

## How to read this report

Three layers are kept separate on purpose:

| Marker | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| **Measured** | Published by Census, Fed, NY Fed, BLS, CDC/NCHS, CMS, or similar |
| **Institute / bank microdata** | JPMorganChase Institute, Gallup Life Evaluation — useful, but sample/selection biased |
| **Estimate (speculation)** | Our affordability ranges and forward scenarios — labeled as such |

If a headline says the economy is healthy while half of workers say they are “struggling,” both can be “true” for different definitions. That gap is the point of this report.

## 1. The picture in one page

1. **Income is extremely skewed.** In 2024 the top fifth of U.S. households took **52.2%** of all household income; the bottom fifth took **3.1%** (U.S. Census Bureau, *Income in the United States: 2024*, P60-286).
2. **Averages lie.** Median household income was **$83,730** — not statistically different from 2023. That number does not describe the bottom or the top.
3. **Debt is large and delinquency is sticky.** Household debt ≈ **$18.8 trillion** (NY Fed, Q1 2026). Credit cards ≈ **$1.25 trillion**. About **4.8%** of all debt balances are in some stage of delinquency; student loans **90+ days delinquent** ≈ **10.3%** of balances.
4. **Cash buffers are thin for many.** Fed SHED 2025: only **63%** would cover a **$400** shock with cash/equivalent; **16%** skipped bills in the prior month; **12%** could not pay $400 by any means.
5. **Health spending is already a fifth of the economy’s future.** CMS: NHE **$5.3 trillion** in 2024 (**18% of GDP**, **$15,474** per person); projected **~20.3% of GDP by 2033**.
6. **Chronic illness is the cost driver.** CDC: in 2023, **76.4%** of adults reported ≥1 chronic condition; **51.4%** reported multiple. CDC also states that people with chronic and mental health conditions account for the large majority of U.S. health spending.
7. **Fertility is far below replacement.** Total fertility rate ≈ **1.62** (final 2023); provisional general fertility rate **53.1** births per 1,000 women 15–44 in 2025 — long-run decline vs. replacement ≈ **2.1**.
8. **“Thriving” is not the majority.** Gallup U.S. adults “thriving” ≈ **48%** in late 2025; among *workers*, **49% struggling / 46% thriving / 5% suffering**. Globally, Gallup’s Life Evaluation Index shows roughly **~31% thriving** — i.e. about **seven in ten** not thriving. That is likely the “~70% just standing up to stay alive” figure you half-remembered — **global**, not U.S.-only.
9. **AI is already uneven.** Fed SHED 2025: **1 in 4** workers used generative AI at work in the prior month; graduate-degree workers more than **4×** as likely as those with high school or less. That is not “AI for everyone.”

**Bottom line (skeptical):** The U.S. can look fine in GDP and stock indices while a large share of households have little room for a new **€100–€500/month** digital service — and almost no room for **€5,000/month**. AI does not automatically reverse that; it can just as easily concentrate capability among the already liquid.

**Why the average is useless here:** The top quintile alone receives more than half of all income. Any “average American can afford X” claim is statistically contaminated by that tail.

| Income quintile (CEX) | Avg annual expenditures |
|-----------------------|-------------------------|
| Lowest | **$35,046** |

**Skeptical note on CEX vs Census:** Lowest-quintile *spending* can approach or exceed reported *income* because of underreporting, transfers, debt, and drawing down savings. That is not proof of hidden wealth; it often means the household is already running hot.

## 3. Who can afford €100 / €200 / €500 / €5,000 per month?

This section is **Estimate (speculation)** built on the measured bands above, plus Fed liquidity indicators. It answers: *could this household pay without immediately skipping rent, food, or medicine?* — not *will they buy your app*.

**Assumptions (stated so you can attack them):**

1. A recurring fee is “affordable” only if the household has **positive discretionary surplus after housing, food, transport, healthcare, and minimum debt service**.
2. Fed SHED: **37%** of adults would *not* cover a **$400** emergency with cash/equivalent; **16%** missed bills last month. Those groups are poor candidates for *any* new €100+ subscription unless they put it on credit.
3. We treat **€5,000/month ($5,500)** as **household** capacity unless noted. In practice that price is almost always a **company** budget, not a family one.
4. “Can afford” ≠ “will pay.” Willingness is far smaller than capacity.

### Rough capacity by band

| Band | €100 / mo (~$110) | €200 / mo (~$220) | €500 / mo (~$550) | €5,000 / mo (~$5,500) |
|------|-------------------|-------------------|-------------------|------------------------|
| **Q1** | Rare; often only via card debt | Effectively no | No | No |
| **Q2** | Minority; fragile | Rare | No | No |
| **Q3** | Possible for many; competes with car repair / kids | Minority | Rare | No |
| **Q4** | Most can | Many can | Minority (discretionary luxury / niche B2C) | Almost no as household |
| **Q5** | Nearly all | Most | Many | Small slice of Q5 |
| **Top ~2–5%** | Yes | Yes | Yes | The realistic B2C pool |

### Headcount-style estimate (U.S. households, ~132 million)

| Monthly price | Households that *could* pay without clear distress | Order of magnitude |
|---------------|----------------------------------------------------|--------------------|
| **€100** | **~35–45%** | **~45–60 million** |
| **€200** | **~20–30%** | **~25–40 million** |
| **€500** | **~8–15%** | **~10–20 million** |
| **€5,000** | **~1–3%** | **~1.5–4 million** |

**Skeptical correction for founders:** The *addressable* market for a premium personal service is usually **much smaller** than “can afford,” because:

- Health, childcare, housing insurance, and debt service rise first (Fed: rent arrears up; 26% skipped medical care due to cost in 2025).
- Credit is already being used as a bridge (NY Fed early credit-card delinquency transition still ~**8.6%** annualized).
- Chase Institute work shows many households *can* cover a one-time $400 shock if you count **credit + redirected spending** — that is **not** the same as adding a permanent €500/month burn.

**For €5,000/month specifically:** That is **$66,000/year** — roughly **twice** the entire annual income ceiling of Q1, and more than many Q2 households earn *before* tax. As a consumer price, you are selling to a thin elite (or to firms). Chasing “the last three rich people” is not a metaphor; at that price it is the business model.

## 4. The “70% don’t strive anymore” claim — what the data actually say

You recalled something like: *70% of people in the world no longer strive; they just stand up to stay alive.*

**Closest measured match (Gallup Life Evaluation Index / Cantril ladder):**

- **Global (2025):** about **31% thriving**, **58% struggling**, **12% suffering** → **~69–70% not thriving**.
- **United States (late 2025):** about **48% thriving** among adults — i.e. a slim majority are *not* in Gallup’s “thriving” bin.
- **U.S. workers (Q4 2025):** **49% struggling**, **46% thriving**, **5% suffering** — first time in Gallup’s worker series that struggling > thriving.

**Do not confuse this with Fed SHED’s “73% doing okay or living comfortably.”**  
Different question, different scale, different incentives. Survey respondents often report being “okay” while rating future life hope lower (Gallup notes the U.S. thriving drop is driven more by **pessimism about five years ahead** than by a collapse in current-life scores).

**Skeptical reading:** Official “doing okay” numbers are soft. Ladder scores and delinquency/cash-buffer data are harder. Use both; trust the harder ones when they conflict.

## 5. Debt: how long can this still rotate?

### Stock of debt (NY Fed Household Debt and Credit, Q1 2026)

| Category | Balance |
|----------|---------|
| Total household debt | **$18.8 trillion** |
| Mortgages | **$13.19 trillion** |
| Auto | **$1.69 trillion** |
| Student loans | **$1.66 trillion** |
| Credit cards | **$1.25 trillion** |
| HELOC | **$446 billion** |
| Other | **$562 billion** |

- **4.8%** of aggregate balances in some stage of delinquency.
- Credit-card transition into *early* delinquency ≈ **8.6%** annualized (slightly down from 8.7%).
- Student loans **90+ days delinquent:** **10.3%** of balances (up from 9.6% prior quarter). Pandemic forbearance ended; defaults are reappearing in the data (NY Fed notes millions transferred toward default resolution).

### Soft savings (BEA)

Personal saving rate ≈ **3.0%** of disposable income (May 2026) — low by historical standards, far below the pandemic spike. That means the system leans on **income + credit**, not a fat cash cushion.

### Fed household stress markers (SHED 2025)

- **16%** did not pay all bills in the prior month  
- **23%** of renters behind on rent at some point in the past year (up vs 2024 and vs 2021)  
- Credit-card balances rose **>35%** (average) among people who said they were “finding it difficult to get by” since 2023  
- **23%** of student-loan borrowers had recent payment difficulty  

**How long can it rotate?**  
Not forever at rising real living costs + low saving + elevated revolving delinquency. The system can keep going if:

1. employment holds,  
2. lenders still extend revolving credit, and  
3. asset owners (housing/stocks) feel rich enough to spend.

If employment softens further (SHED: job-finding concern up to **42%** of adults; more under-30s cite inability to find work), the revolving layer (cards, auto, BNPL) breaks before the mortgage layer for most people. That is the usual sequence — not a prophecy, a credit-cycle pattern.

**Skeptical note:** Aggregate delinquency “only 4.8%” sounds calm because **mortgages dominate the denominator**. Credit cards and student loans are the early-warning instruments; they are already louder than the headline aggregate.

## 6. Health: sicker people, larger bill

### Disease burden (CDC)

- **76.4%** of U.S. adults (≈ **194 million**) reported ≥1 chronic condition in 2023; **51.4%** (≈ **130 million**) reported multiple chronic conditions (BRFSS analysis, CDC *Preventing Chronic Disease*, 2025).
- Among **young** adults, ≥1 chronic condition rose from **52.5% (2013)** to **59.5% (2023)**; multiple conditions from **21.8%** to **27.1%**.
- Obesity ≈ **40.3%** of adults (NHANES Aug 2021–Aug 2023); severe obesity up to ≈ **9.7%**.
- Total diabetes ≈ **15.8%** of adults (same NHANES window); age-adjusted prevalence has risen since 1999–2000.

### Cost (CMS National Health Expenditure)

- **2024:** **$5.3 trillion**, **$15,474 per person**, **18.0% of GDP** (growth **+7.2%**).
- **Projection to 2033:** ~**$8.6 trillion**, ~**20.3% of GDP**, ~**$24,200 per person** (CMS Office of the Actuary / secondary summaries of the same forecast).

**Household meaning:** Even insured families face premiums, deductibles, and skipped care (**26%** of adults skipped medical care due to cost in 2025, Fed SHED). A €200 lifestyle app competes with a dental bill and a deductible — and usually loses.

**Skeptical note:** “People are getting sicker” is **partly age structure** (population aging) and **partly true morbidity** (obesity, diabetes, depression in younger cohorts). Both raise the medical claim on income. You do not need a conspiracy to see the budget squeeze.

## 7. Birth rates: fewer children, older country

| Metric | Value | Source |
|--------|-------|--------|
| Births 2023 (final) | **3,596,017** (−2% vs 2022) | CDC NCHS NVSS |
| Total fertility rate 2023 | **1.621** births per woman | CDC NCHS |
| TFR 2024 (provisional) | **~1.627** | CDC VSRR |
| General fertility rate 2025 (provisional) | **53.1** per 1,000 women 15–44 (−1% vs 2024) | CDC VSRR No. 043 |
| Replacement-level TFR (rule of thumb) | **~2.1** | Demography standard |

**Where that leads (measured + short inference):**

- Fewer young entrants into the labor force over decades → higher dependency ratio (more retirees per worker).
- Political and fiscal pressure on Social Security / Medicare grows (already visible in CMS Medicare growth assumptions).
- Housing and school markets reprice unevenly; immigration becomes the main swing factor for population growth — contested, not automatic.

**For consumer businesses:** The “young upwardly mobile subscriber forever” funnel shrinks. The money that remains concentrates in older households and in the top income bands — again pushing premium services toward the top.

## 8. AI: turnaround for the many, or tool for the few?

### What is measured

- Fed SHED 2025: **25%** of workers used generative AI at work in the prior month.
- Use is **strongly education-skewed** (graduate degree >> high school).
- Users mostly say it **saves time**; they are more likely to expect career *help* than job *replacement* — but that is the view of people **already using it**, i.e. selection bias.

### What is not measured (and should stay labeled Estimate)

| Optimistic story | Skeptical counter |
|------------------|-------------------|
| AI lifts productivity → wages → everyone can pay for services | Productivity gains often accrue to capital owners and scarce complementary skills first |
| AI apps at €100–€500/mo scale to the mass market | Mass market cash buffers are thin; delinquency and medical costs absorb surplus |
| AI needs ever more energy/compute → growth for all | Energy and capex are paid by someone; if households cannot pay heating, they will not fund your GPU bill either |
| “AI will save the economy” | Same phrase was used for the internet, fracking, and zero rates. Sometimes true for GDP; often false for the median household |

**Your electricity metaphor is directionally right as economics, even if the politics are messy:**  
Training and serving frontier models is **capital- and energy-intensive**. If only a thin top of the income distribution (and large firms) can pay for the best models, you get **AI stratification**: cheap/weak tools for the many, expensive/strong tools for the few. That is compatible with “AI everywhere” marketing and with “AI for the 2%” outcomes at the same time.

**Is there a chance AI turns it around for broad America?**  
Yes, *if* productivity gains show up as **lower prices for necessities**, **higher real median wages**, or **public infrastructure** (energy, health admin, education) — not only as SaaS seats for consultants.  
As of mid-2026, the hard household data (saving rate, card stress, medical cost share, thriving scores) do **not** yet show that broad turnaround. Betting the company on “everyone will pay €500/month for AI” is betting against the income distribution.

## 9. Two Americas in the same spreadsheet

For someone who has never looked at these tables (stable public job, believes official calm):

| Story you hear | What the tables show |
|----------------|----------------------|
| “Median income is fine” | Median flat; **top fifth owns half the income flow** |
| “Consumers are resilient” | Resilience often = **credit** (Chase: credit + redirected spend covers shocks; Fed: balances up most for those “finding it difficult”) |
| “Only 4.8% of debt is delinquent” | Mortgages dilute the average; **cards & student loans** are worse |
| “73% are doing okay” | Gallup: **~half not thriving**; workers: **struggling > thriving** |
| “AI will create millions of customers” | AI use already **skews to the educated**; disposable cash skews to the top |
| “Health insurance means health is handled” | **18% of GDP** and rising; **¼** skip care for cost |

Anecdotes from Europe (logistics fleets collapsing from ~100 trucks to ~7, freelancers without projects for months) are **not** U.S. Census cells — but they rhyme with the U.S. microdata: thin buffers, rising fixed costs, and a product economy that prices for the top.

## 10. Forward scenarios (explicitly speculative)

Horizon: roughly the next 5–10 years. Not forecasts — coherent stories constrained by the data above.

### A — Soft muddle (most likely base)

Employment wobbles but does not collapse. Card delinquency stays elevated. Health share of GDP drifts toward **20%**. Fertility stays near **1.6**. AI revenue concentrates in enterprise and top-quintile consumers. Many €100 apps live; few €500 consumer apps do; €5,000 is B2B.

### B — Credit bite

Job-finding stress worsens; revolving credit tightens. Subscriptions and discretionary services are cut first. Aggregate GDP can still look okay while **unit economics of consumer AI SaaS break**.

### C — Stratified AI boom

Energy + chip buildout succeeds for capital owners. Frontier AI becomes a **utility for firms and the top ~2–5%**. Public-facing “AI for all” is a thinner, ad-supported, or employer-gated layer. Politically unstable, economically plausible.

### D — Broad productivity pass-through (the hopeful one)

AI + policy actually lowers real costs of housing admin, healthcare billing, education, and energy. Median real discretionary income rises. Then €100–€200 services become mass again. **This is possible. It is not the default of the current tables.**

## 11. Practical implications (if you build or sell services)

1. **Price to the band you can actually reach.** €5,000/month = elite/enterprise. €500/month = upper-middle + top. €100/month still fights groceries and debt.
2. **Do not use “average income” in a pitch deck.** Use quintiles or the top 20% / top 5%.
3. **Count medical and housing inflation as your competitors.** They already won share of wallet.
4. **If your plan needs “everyone on AI subscriptions,” your plan needs a different country than the one in Census P60-286.**
5. **Skepticism cuts both ways:** doom without numbers is as useless as hopium. Update when NY Fed delinquency, BEA saving, and Census real incomes move.

## Closing (plain language)

America is not “broke” as a country. It is **uneven**. A large minority is one bill behind; a large middle has little room for new monthly luxury; a small top has most of the income flow and most of the capacity for expensive AI. Birth rates are low, chronic disease and health spending are high, and revolving credit is already showing stress. AI can amplify that split faster than it heals it — unless productivity shows up as **cheaper necessities and higher median real surplus**, which the current household data do not yet show.

If someone with a calm salary and a government job says “it can’t be that bad,” hand them the Census quintile table and the NY Fed credit-card page. The argument is not vibes. It is who has money left after rent, medicine, and interest.
